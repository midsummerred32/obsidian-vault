## Concept 4.3: The eukaryotic cell's genetic instructions are housed in the nucleus and carried out by the ribosomes

Two components involved in genetic control: the **nucleus** (houses most DNA) and **ribosomes** (use DNA's information to make proteins).

### The Nucleus: Information Central
- Contains most of the cell's genes. (Some genes are in mitochondria and chloroplasts.)
- Usually the most conspicuous organelle; averages ~**5 µm** in diameter.
- **Nuclear envelope** encloses the nucleus, separating it from the cytoplasm:
  - **Double membrane**; each membrane is a lipid bilayer with proteins.
  - Membranes are separated by a **20–40 nm** space.
  - Perforated by **pores ~100 nm** in diameter. At each pore's lip, the inner and outer membranes are continuous.
  - A **pore complex** (protein structure) lines each pore. It regulates entry/exit of **proteins, RNAs, and large macromolecular complexes**.
- **Nuclear lamina:** netlike array of protein filaments lining the nuclear side of the envelope (except at pores). It maintains nuclear shape by mechanically supporting the envelope.

### Chromosomes and chromatin
- DNA is organized into **chromosomes**. Each has **one long DNA molecule** associated with many proteins.
- Some proteins help **coil** the DNA, shortening it so it fits in the nucleus.
- **Chromatin** = the complex of DNA and proteins making up chromosomes.
- **Non-dividing cell:** chromatin appears as a diffuse mass; individual chromosomes can't be distinguished (though they are present).
- **Cell preparing to divide:** chromosomes coil (condense) further and become thick enough to see as separate structures.
- Each species has a characteristic chromosome number. Humans: **46** in typical cells; **23** in sex cells (eggs and sperm).

### The Nucleolus
- Prominent structure in a non-dividing nucleus: dense granules and fibers next to part of the chromatin. There may be 2 or more.
- **rRNA (ribosomal RNA)** is synthesized here from DNA instructions.
- Proteins imported from the cytoplasm are assembled with rRNA into **large and small ribosomal subunits**.
- Subunits exit through the nuclear pores; a large and a small subunit assemble into a ribosome in the cytoplasm.

### How the nucleus directs protein synthesis
1. Nucleus makes **mRNA** according to DNA instructions.
2. mRNA is transported to the cytoplasm via nuclear pores.
3. **Ribosomes translate** the mRNA message into the **primary structure** of a specific polypeptide. (Details in Ch. 14.)

### Ribosomes: Protein Factories
- Complexes of **rRNA + protein** that carry out protein synthesis.
- **Not membrane-bound**, so they are *not* considered organelles.
- Cells with high protein synthesis have many ribosomes and prominent nucleoli. Example: a human pancreas cell has a few million ribosomes.

| | Free ribosomes | Bound ribosomes |
|---|---|---|
| **Location** | Suspended in cytosol | Attached to outside of ER or nuclear envelope |
| **Proteins made** | Function within the cytosol (e.g., enzymes for early sugar breakdown) | Proteins for insertion into **membranes**, packaging in organelles (e.g., **lysosomes**), or **export/secretion** |

- Free and bound ribosomes are **structurally identical** and can switch roles.
- Secretory cells (e.g., pancreas cells that secrete digestive enzymes) often have a high proportion of bound ribosomes.

## Concept 4.4: The endomembrane system regulates protein traffic and performs metabolic functions in the cell

### Overview
- **Endomembrane system** includes: nuclear envelope, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, various vesicles and vacuoles, and the plasma membrane.
- Tasks: protein synthesis; transport of proteins into membranes/organelles or out of the cell; lipid metabolism and movement; **detoxification of poisons**.
- Membranes are related by **direct physical continuity** or by **transfer of membrane segments as vesicles**.
- Despite this, membranes are **not identical**. Thickness, composition, and reactions can change several times during a membrane's life.

### The Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER): Biosynthetic Factory
- So extensive it can make up **more than half** of a eukaryotic cell's total membrane. (*Endoplasmic* = "within the cytoplasm"; *reticulum* = "little net.")
- Network of membranous tubules and sacs called **cisternae**.
- The ER membrane encloses the **ER lumen** (cisternal space), separating it from the cytosol.
- ER membrane is **continuous with the nuclear envelope**, so the space between the envelope's two membranes is continuous with the ER lumen.

| | Smooth ER | Rough ER |
|---|---|---|
| **Surface** | No ribosomes | Studded with ribosomes (outer surface) |
| **Main roles** | Lipid synthesis, carbohydrate metabolism, detoxification, calcium storage | Secretory protein synthesis, membrane production |

- Ribosomes also attach to the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope, which is continuous with rough ER.

#### Functions of Smooth ER
- **Lipid synthesis:** oils, steroids, and new membrane phospholipids.
  - Includes vertebrate **sex hormones** and adrenal steroid hormones.
  - Cells making these hormones (testes, ovaries) are rich in smooth ER: structure fits function.
- **Detoxification** (especially liver cells):
  - Usually involves **adding hydroxyl groups** to drugs, making them more soluble and easier to flush out.
  - Examples: phenobarbital and other barbiturates.
  - Barbiturates, alcohol, and many drugs **induce proliferation of smooth ER** and its detox enzymes, which increases **tolerance** (higher doses needed).
  - Detox enzymes have broad action, so proliferation from one drug can raise dosage needs for **other drugs too** (e.g., barbiturate abuse can reduce the effectiveness of some antibiotics).
- **Calcium ion storage:** in muscle cells, smooth ER pumps Ca²⁺ from cytosol into the ER lumen. When a nerve impulse stimulates the cell, Ca²⁺ rushes back into the cytosol and **triggers muscle contraction**.

#### Functions of Rough ER
- **Secretory proteins** (e.g., **insulin** from certain pancreatic cells):
  - The growing polypeptide is **threaded into the ER lumen** through a pore formed by a protein complex in the ER membrane.
  - It folds into its functional shape in the lumen.
  - Most secretory proteins are **glycoproteins** (carbohydrates covalently attached by enzymes in the ER membrane).
  - The ER membrane keeps secretory proteins separate from cytosolic proteins made by free ribosomes.
  - They leave the ER in **transport vesicles** that bud from **transitional ER**.
- **Membrane factory:**
  - Grows by adding membrane proteins and phospholipids to its own membrane.
  - Membrane proteins are inserted into the ER membrane and anchored by their **hydrophobic** portions.
  - Phospholipids are assembled by ER enzymes from cytosolic precursors.
  - Portions of the ER membrane are transferred as transport vesicles to other endomembrane components.

### The Golgi Apparatus: Shipping and Receiving Center
- A "warehouse" for **receiving, sorting, shipping, and some manufacturing**. Products of the ER (e.g., proteins) are **modified and stored**, then sent onward.
- Especially extensive in **secretory cells**.
- Structure: stacks of flattened membranous sacs (**cisternae**), like **pita bread**. A cell may have hundreds of stacks. Unlike ER cisternae, Golgi cisternae are **not physically connected**.
- **Polarity:**
  - **cis face** = "receiving" side, usually near the ER. ER vesicles fuse here.
  - **trans face** = "shipping" side. Vesicles pinch off and travel to other sites.
  - Membranes on the two sides differ in thickness and molecular composition.
- **Modification:** ER products are modified from cis to trans.
  - Glycoprotein carbohydrates are modified first in the ER, then in the Golgi. The Golgi removes some sugar monomers and substitutes others, giving many carbohydrate variants.
  - Membrane phospholipids may also be altered.
- **Manufacturing:** makes some macromolecules, e.g., **pectins** and other non-cellulose polysaccharides in plant cells, which are incorporated into cell walls with cellulose.
- **Cisternal maturation model:** cisternae themselves progress **cis → trans**, carrying and modifying cargo. Some vesicles recycle enzymes backward to less mature cisternae. This replaces the older view of the Golgi as a static structure with vesicles moving products between fixed cisternae.
- **Sorting and targeting:**
  - Molecular tags (e.g., **phosphate groups**) work like **zip codes** on mailing labels.
  - Vesicles have external molecules that recognize **docking sites** on target organelles or the plasma membrane.

### Lysosomes: Digestive Compartments
- Membranous sac of **hydrolytic enzymes** used to digest (hydrolyze) macromolecules.
- Enzymes work best in the **acidic** lysosome interior. If a lysosome leaks, the enzymes are not very active in the near-neutral cytosol. However, excessive leakage from many lysosomes can destroy the cell by **self-digestion**.
- Enzymes and lysosomal membrane are made by **rough ER**, then processed in the Golgi. At least some lysosomes bud from the **trans face** of the Golgi.
- Lysosomal proteins are spared from digestion probably because their **3-D shapes protect vulnerable bonds**.

**Phagocytosis** (Fig. 4.12)
- Cell engulfs smaller organisms or food particles, forming a **food vacuole** that fuses with a lysosome, whose enzymes digest the contents.
- Products (simple sugars, amino acids, other monomers) pass into the cytosol as nutrients.
- Used by amoebas and other unicellular eukaryotes. In humans, **macrophages** (white blood cells) engulf and destroy bacteria and other invaders.

**Autophagy** (Fig. 4.13)
- Lysosomes **recycle the cell's own material**.
- A damaged organelle or bit of cytosol is surrounded by a **double membrane**; a lysosome fuses with the outer membrane, enzymes break down the contents, and the small compounds are released to the cytosol for reuse.
- Example: a human liver cell recycles about **half its macromolecules each week**.

**Lysosomal storage diseases**
- Inherited; cells lack a functioning lysosomal hydrolytic enzyme, so lysosomes fill with indigestible material.
- **Tay-Sachs disease:** a lipid-digesting enzyme is missing or inactive, so lipids accumulate in brain cells.
- Rare in the general population.

### Vacuoles: Diverse Maintenance Compartments
- **Large vesicles derived from the ER and Golgi**, so part of the endomembrane system.
- Selective membrane, so vacuole contents differ from the cytosol.

| Type | Function |
|---|---|
| **Food vacuole** | Formed by phagocytosis; digested by a lysosome |
| **Contractile vacuole** | In freshwater unicellular eukaryotes; pumps out excess water to maintain ion/molecule concentration |
| **Hydrolytic vacuole** | In plants and fungi; enzymatic hydrolysis (like lysosomes in animals; some call them a type of lysosome) |
| **Storage vacuole** | Small plant vacuoles holding reserves (e.g., seed proteins) |
| **Defense vacuole** | Stores poisonous or unpalatable compounds against herbivores |
| **Pigment vacuole** | Red/blue petal pigments that attract pollinators |

**Central vacuole (mature plant cells)**
- Forms by coalescence of smaller vacuoles.
- Contents = **cell sap**, the main repository of inorganic ions (e.g., K⁺, Cl⁻).
- Plant cells **grow by absorbing water into the vacuole**, enlarging with minimal new cytoplasm.
- Cytosol is often a thin layer between the vacuole and plasma membrane, so the membrane-surface-to-cytosolic-volume ratio stays adequate even for large plant cells.

### The Endomembrane System: Review (Fig. 4.15)
- Membrane flows: **nuclear envelope ↔ rough ER ↔ smooth ER → transport vesicles → Golgi → lysosomes, other vesicles, vacuoles, plasma membrane**.
- Transport vesicles carry proteins to the plasma membrane for secretion, and the plasma membrane expands by vesicle fusion.
- Membrane composition and function are modified as it moves ER → Golgi → elsewhere.
- Lysosomes are available to fuse with other vesicles for digestion.

## Concept 4.5: Mitochondria and chloroplasts change energy from one form to another

- **Mitochondria:** sites of **cellular respiration**. Use oxygen to drive **ATP** generation by extracting energy from sugars, fats, and other fuels.
- **Chloroplasts** (plants and algae): sites of **photosynthesis**. Convert solar energy to chemical energy, using sunlight to synthesize organic compounds (sugars) from CO₂ and water.
- Related functions and similar evolutionary origins.
- **Peroxisome** also covered here. Its evolutionary origin and relation to other organelles are still debated.

### Evolutionary Origins: Endosymbiont Theory (Fig. 4.16)
- An early ancestor of eukaryotic cells (the host) **engulfed an oxygen-using, nonphotosynthetic prokaryote**. It became an **endosymbiont** (a cell living within another cell), and over evolutionary time host and endosymbiont merged into one organism: a eukaryote with a **mitochondrion**.
- At least one such cell then took up a **photosynthetic prokaryote**, becoming the ancestor of cells with **chloroplasts**.
- Widely accepted (discussed further in Concept 25.1).

**Supporting evidence**
1. **Double membranes:** unlike endomembrane organelles (single membrane), both have two. The ancestral engulfed prokaryotes probably had two outer membranes.
2. **Ribosomes and circular DNA:** like prokaryotes, they have ribosomes and multiple **circular DNA** molecules associated with their inner membranes. Their DNA programs synthesis of some organelle proteins on ribosomes assembled there.
3. **Autonomy:** they grow and reproduce independently within the cell.

### Mitochondria: Chemical Energy Conversion (Fig. 4.17)
- Found in nearly all eukaryotic cells (plants, animals, fungi, most unicellular eukaryotes).
- Number ranges from one large mitochondrion to hundreds or thousands per cell, and **correlates with metabolic activity** (moving or contracting cells have more per volume).
- Size: **1–10 µm** long.
- Structure:
  - Two membranes, each a phospholipid bilayer with its own proteins.
  - **Outer membrane:** smooth.
  - **Inner membrane:** convoluted, with infoldings called **cristae**.
  - **Intermembrane space:** narrow region between the membranes.
  - **Mitochondrial matrix:** enclosed by the inner membrane. Contains enzymes, mitochondrial DNA, and ribosomes.
- Matrix enzymes catalyze some steps of respiration. Other respiratory proteins, including **ATP synthase**, are built into the inner membrane.
- **Cristae give a large surface area**, boosting respiration (structure fits function).
- Dynamic: living cells show mitochondria moving, changing shape, **fusing or dividing**. They form a **branched tubular network**. (Ch. 7 covers respiration.)

### Chloroplasts: Capture of Light Energy (Fig. 4.18)
- Contain green pigment **chlorophyll**, plus enzymes and other molecules for photosynthetic sugar production.
- Lens-shaped, about **3–6 µm** long; found in leaves and other green plant organs and in algae.
- Structure:
  - **Envelope** of two membranes separated by a very narrow intermembrane space.
  - **Thylakoids:** flattened, interconnected sacs inside. In some regions they are stacked like poker chips, each stack a **granum** (plural, *grana*).
  - **Stroma:** fluid outside the thylakoids. Contains chloroplast DNA, ribosomes, and many enzymes.
- **Three compartments:** intermembrane space, stroma, thylakoid space. This organization lets the chloroplast convert light to chemical energy. (Ch. 8 covers photosynthesis.)
- Dynamic: shape changes, they grow and **pinch in two**, and they move along **cytoskeletal tracks** like mitochondria and other organelles.

### Plastids
Chloroplasts belong to a family of related plant organelles called **plastids**:

| Plastid | Function |
|---|---|
| **Chloroplast** | Photosynthesis |
| **Amyloplast** | Colorless; stores **starch (amylose)**, esp. in roots and tubers |
| **Chromoplast** | Pigments that give fruits and flowers orange/yellow colors |

### Peroxisomes: Oxidation (Fig. 4.19)
- Specialized **metabolic compartment** bounded by a **single membrane**. Roughly spherical, often with a granular or crystalline core (dense enzyme collection).
- Enzymes remove hydrogen atoms from certain molecules and transfer them to **O₂**, producing **hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂)**.
- Example: liver peroxisomes **detoxify alcohol** and other harmful compounds this way.
- H₂O₂ is toxic, but peroxisomes also contain an enzyme that **converts H₂O₂ → water**.
- **Key idea:** enzymes that make H₂O₂ and those that dispose of it are **sequestered** from other components that could be damaged. This shows how compartmentalization is crucial to function.
- Grow by importing proteins made in the cytosol and ER, plus lipids from the ER and from within the peroxisome.
- Open questions: how they increase in number and how they arose in evolution.
- Cooperate with chloroplasts and mitochondria in certain metabolic functions.

## Concept 4.6 (pp. 90–91 only): The cytoskeleton is a network of fibers that organizes structures and activities in the cell

### What it is
- A **network of fibers extending throughout the cytoplasm** (Fig. 4.20).
- Early electron microscopy led biologists to think organelles **floated freely** in the cytosol. Improved light and electron microscopy revealed the cytoskeleton.

### Roles: Support and Motility

**Support / shape**
- Gives **mechanical support** and maintains cell shape, especially important in **animal cells, which lack walls**.
- Strength and resilience come from its architecture: like a **dome tent**, it is stabilized by a balance of opposing forces from its elements.
- Provides **anchorage** for many organelles and even cytosolic enzymes.
- More **dynamic** than an animal skeleton: it can be quickly dismantled in one place and reassembled elsewhere, **changing cell shape**.

**Motility (movement)**
- **Cell motility** includes changes in cell **location** and more limited movements of **cell parts**.
- Generally requires the cytoskeleton to interact with **motor proteins**.
- Examples:
  - With plasma membrane molecules, allows whole cells to move along fibers **outside** the cell.
  - Inside the cell, **vesicles and organelles "walk"** on motor protein "feet" along cytoskeletal tracks (Fig. 4.21).
  - Neurotransmitter-containing vesicles travel to the tips of **axons** this way.
  - The cytoskeleton **bends the plasma membrane inward** to form food vacuoles or other phagocytic vesicles.
- Motor proteins are **ATP-powered** and attach to vesicle receptors. They walk along **microtubules** (sometimes **microfilaments**).

### Components of the Cytoskeleton
Three main fiber types by thickness: **microtubules (thickest)** > **intermediate filaments (middle)** > **microfilaments (thinnest)**.

**Table 4.1: Structure and Function of the Cytoskeleton**

| Property | Microtubules | Microfilaments (Actin Filaments) | Intermediate Filaments |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Structure** | Hollow tubes | Two intertwined strands of actin | Fibrous proteins coiled into cables |
| **Diameter** | 25 nm (15-nm lumen) | 7 nm | 8–12 nm |
| **Protein subunits** | **Tubulin** (dimer of α-tubulin + β-tubulin) | **Actin** | One of several proteins (e.g., **keratins**) |
| **Main functions** | Cell shape; cell motility; chromosome movement in cell division; organelle movement | Cell shape; changes in cell shape; muscle contraction; cytoplasmic streaming (plants); cell motility; cell division (animals) | Cell shape; anchorage of nucleus and certain other organelles; formation of nuclear lamina |

## Quick Review
- **4.3:** Nucleus (envelope, pores, lamina, chromatin, nucleolus) holds the DNA; ribosomes (free vs. bound) make proteins from mRNA.
- **4.4:** Endomembrane system = nuclear envelope, ER (smooth/rough), Golgi (cis → trans), lysosomes, vesicles, vacuoles, plasma membrane. Connected by direct continuity or vesicle transport.
- **4.5:** Mitochondria (respiration; cristae, matrix) and chloroplasts (photosynthesis; thylakoids, grana, stroma) arose by endosymbiosis. Peroxisomes make and break down H₂O₂.
- **4.6:** Cytoskeleton = support + motility. Three fibers: microtubules, microfilaments, intermediate filaments. Motor proteins use ATP to move cargo along the tracks.
