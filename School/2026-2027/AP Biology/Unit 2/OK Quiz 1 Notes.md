## Concept 4.1 — Biologists use microscopes and the tools of biochemistry to study cells

### Microscopy

**Three key parameters:**
- **Magnification** — ratio of an image's size to the object's real size
- **Resolution** — measure of image clarity; the minimum distance two points can be apart and still be seen as separate. Light microscopes can't resolve anything finer than ~0.2 µm (200 nm)
- **Contrast** — difference in brightness between light and dark areas of an image

**Light microscope (LM):** visible light passes through the specimen, then through glass lenses that refract (bend) the light to magnify the image.
- Effective magnification limit: ~1,000×
- Types (Figure 4.3):
  - *Brightfield (unstained)* — light passes straight through; little contrast unless cells are naturally pigmented
  - *Brightfield (stained)* — dyes enhance contrast but usually kill/fix the cells
  - *Phase-contrast* — amplifies density variations; good for living, unpigmented cells
  - *Differential-interference contrast (Nomarski)* — exaggerates density differences; image looks 3-D
  - *Fluorescence* — fluorescent dyes/antibodies label specific molecules, which glow
  - *Confocal* — uses a laser to eliminate out-of-focus light ("optical sectioning"), producing sharp single-plane images that can be reconstructed in 3-D

**Electron microscope (EM):** focuses a beam of electrons through or onto the specimen. Electron wavelengths are much shorter than visible light, so resolution is far better (modern EMs can theoretically resolve ~0.002 nm, practically ~2 nm — a 100-fold improvement over light microscopes).
- **Scanning electron microscope (SEM):** beam scans the specimen's surface (coated in a thin film of gold); shows detailed 3-D surface topography
- **Transmission electron microscope (TEM):** beam passes through a thin section of specimen stained with heavy metals; used to study internal structures
- Both use electromagnets as lenses instead of glass

**Trade-offs:** EM reveals subcellular structures invisible to LM, but specimen preparation kills the cells and can create *artifacts* (structural features seen in micrographs that don't exist in the living cell). LM allows study of living cells, and modern techniques (fluorescent labeling, confocal, super-resolution microscopy) now resolve structures as small as 10–20 nm.

- **Cytology** = study of cell structure; **biochemistry** = study of cell metabolism (chemical processes). Understanding cells required integrating both.

### Cell Fractionation

- Technique that **disrupts cells and separates major organelles/components by centrifugation**, so they can be studied in bulk.
- Progressively higher-speed spins pellet larger components first (nuclei → mitochondria/chloroplasts → membrane fragments → ribosomes, smallest).
- Lets researchers correlate **structure with function** — e.g., biochemical tests showed respiratory enzymes in a fraction that EM revealed was rich in mitochondria, helping establish mitochondria as the site of cellular respiration.

## Concept 4.2 — Eukaryotic cells have internal membranes that compartmentalize their functions

### Prokaryotic vs. Eukaryotic Cells

All cells share:
- A **plasma membrane** (selective barrier)
- **Cytosol** — semifluid, jellylike substance where subcellular components are suspended
- **Chromosomes** carrying genes (DNA)
- **Ribosomes** that make proteins

Key difference — location of DNA:
- **Eukaryotic cell:** most DNA is in the **nucleus**, bounded by a double membrane
- **Prokaryotic cell:** DNA is concentrated in a region called the **nucleoid**, which is *not* membrane-enclosed

(*Eukaryotic* = "true nucleus"; *prokaryotic* = "before nucleus.")

- **Cytoplasm** = everything inside the plasma membrane; in eukaryotes, specifically the region between the nucleus and plasma membrane, containing membrane-bound organelles suspended in cytosol.
- Prokaryotes lack membrane-bound organelles but aren't a "formless soup" — some have protein-bounded regions for specific reactions.

### Cell Size

- Eukaryotic cells are generally much larger than prokaryotic cells.
- Smallest known cells: mycoplasmas (bacteria), 0.1–1.0 µm diameter
- Typical bacteria: 1–5 µm
- Typical eukaryotic cells: 10–100 µm

**Surface area-to-volume ratio** limits cell size:
- The plasma membrane can only move a limited amount of material per second per unit area, so the **ratio of surface area to volume** is critical for exchanging materials with the environment.
- As an object grows, volume increases faster than surface area (surface area ∝ length², volume ∝ length³) — so **larger cells have proportionally less surface area per unit volume**.
- This is why larger organisms have *more* cells rather than *larger* cells, and why cells needing high exchange rates (e.g., intestinal cells) have adaptations like **microvilli** to increase surface area without increasing volume much.

### The Plasma Membrane

- Structure: a **double layer (bilayer) of phospholipids**, with proteins embedded in or attached to it.
  - Hydrophobic tails face the interior of the membrane
  - Hydrophilic heads face the aqueous solutions on either side
  - Carbohydrate side chains may attach to proteins/lipids on the outer surface
- Same basic bilayer structure applies to organelle membranes too.
- Each membrane type has a unique composition of lipids and proteins suited to its specific function (e.g., respiration enzymes embedded in mitochondrial membranes).

### A Panoramic View of the Eukaryotic Cell

- Eukaryotic cells have extensive internal membranes that divide the cell into **compartments (organelles)**, each providing a distinct local environment for specific metabolic functions — allowing incompatible processes to occur simultaneously in one cell.

**Animal cell organelles (Figure 4.7):**
- **Nucleus** — nuclear envelope (double membrane, perforated by pores), nucleolus (makes ribosomes), chromatin (DNA + proteins)
- **Endoplasmic reticulum (ER)** — network of membranous sacs/tubes; *rough ER* (ribosome-studded) and *smooth ER*
- **Ribosomes** — make proteins; free in cytosol or bound to rough ER/nuclear envelope
- **Golgi apparatus** — synthesis, modification, sorting, and secretion of cell products
- **Lysosome** — digestive organelle; hydrolyzes macromolecules
- **Mitochondrion** — site of cellular respiration; generates most ATP
- **Peroxisome** — specialized metabolic organelle; produces then breaks down hydrogen peroxide
- **Cytoskeleton** — network of protein fibers (microfilaments, intermediate filaments, microtubules) that reinforces cell shape and functions in movement
- **Centrosome** — region where microtubules are initiated; contains a pair of centrioles
- **Flagellum** — motility structure in some animal cells
- **Microvilli** — projections that increase surface area
- **Plasma membrane** — encloses the cell

**Plant cell — key differences from animal cells (Figure 4.7):**
- Has a **cell wall** (cellulose, other polysaccharides, protein) outside the plasma membrane, for shape and protection
- Has a large **central vacuole** — storage, waste breakdown, macromolecule hydrolysis; its enlargement drives plant cell growth
- Has **chloroplasts** — photosynthetic organelles that convert light energy into chemical energy (sugars)
- Has **plasmodesmata** — cytoplasmic channels through cell walls connecting adjacent cells
- Lacks centrioles/flagella (in most plant cells) and lysosomes/peroxisomes are less emphasized in the diagram, but plant cells still have mitochondria, ER, Golgi, ribosomes, cytoskeleton, and peroxisomes
