## Intro: Life at the Edge

- The **plasma membrane** is the boundary between the living cell and its surroundings.
- It is only ~**8 nm thick** (>8,000 membranes stacked ≈ one sheet of paper).
- Like all biological membranes, it shows **selective permeability**: some substances cross more easily than others.
- This ability to discriminate chemical exchange is fundamental to life.
- **Aquaporin** example: one protein lets billions of water molecules cross per second, far more than could cross unaided.

# Concept 5.1: Cellular membranes are fluid mosaics of lipids and proteins

## Basic Structure

- Main ingredients: **lipids** and **proteins** (carbohydrates also important).
- Most abundant lipids: **phospholipids**.
- A phospholipid is **amphipathic**: has both a
  - **hydrophilic** region (phosphate head), and
  - **hydrophobic** region (hydrocarbon tails).
- **Phospholipid bilayer**: stable boundary between two aqueous compartments.
  - Hydrophobic tails are sheltered from water (face inward).
  - Hydrophilic heads face water (cytosol and extracellular fluid).
- **Fluid mosaic model**: the membrane is a *mosaic* of proteins "bobbing" in a *fluid* bilayer of phospholipids.
  - Proteins are **not** randomly distributed; groups form long-lasting specialized patches (as do certain lipids).
  - It's a model, continually refined by new research.
- Most membrane proteins are also amphipathic:
  - Hydrophilic regions contact water; hydrophobic parts sit in the nonaqueous bilayer interior.

---

## The Fluidity of Membranes

- Membranes are **not static**; held together mainly by **hydrophobic interactions** (much weaker than covalent bonds).
- **Lateral movement** (within the plane of the membrane):
  - Phospholipids: **rapid**.
  - Proteins: larger, so **slower**; some drift, some move in a directed way (driven along cytoskeletal fibers by motor proteins).
  - Many proteins are **immobile**, anchored to the cytoskeleton or the extracellular matrix (ECM).

### Experiment (Fig. 5.4): Do membrane proteins move?
- **Frye & Edidin**: labeled mouse and human cell membrane proteins with two different markers, then fused the cells.
- **Result**: after 1 hour the markers were mixed across the hybrid cell.
- **Conclusion**: at least some membrane proteins move sideways in the membrane.
- *Caution:* if proteins did *not* mix, you couldn't conclude proteins don't move (they could be anchored to the cytoskeleton, etc.).

### Factors affecting fluidity (Fig. 5.5)

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Temperature drop** | Membrane stays fluid until phospholipids pack tightly and it **solidifies** (like bacon grease → lard) |
| **Unsaturated tails** | **Kinks** at double bonds prevent tight packing → **more fluid**, stays fluid to lower temps |
| **Saturated tails** | Straight, pack closely → **less fluid** |
| **Cholesterol** (animal cells) | Acts as a **fluidity buffer** (see below) |

**Cholesterol (steroid wedged between phospholipids):**
- At **high temps** (e.g., 37 °C body temp): **reduces fluidity** by restraining phospholipid movement.
- At **low temps**: **hinders solidification** by disrupting regular packing → lowers the solidification temperature.
- **Net effect:** helps membranes **resist changes in fluidity** when temperature changes.

### Why fluidity matters
- Membranes are normally about as fluid as **salad oil**.
- If a membrane **solidifies**: permeability changes, and enzymatic proteins may become inactive.
- If it's **too fluid**: proteins can't function properly either.
- So extreme environments are a challenge → evolutionary adaptations in lipid composition.

---

## Evolution of Differences in Membrane Lipid Composition

- Lipid composition variation = **adaptation to maintain appropriate fluidity** in specific environments.
- Examples:
  - **Cold-water fishes**: high proportion of **unsaturated** tails, so membranes stay fluid.
  - **Hot-spring bacteria/archaea** (>90 °C): unusual lipids that **prevent excessive fluidity**.
  - **Winter wheat**: % unsaturated phospholipids **increases in autumn** so membranes don't solidify in winter.
  - Some bacteria/archaea adjust unsaturated phospholipid proportion based on growth temperature.
- Natural selection favors organisms whose lipid mix gives the right fluidity for their environment.

## Membrane Proteins and Their Functions

- The membrane is a **mosaic**: collage of different proteins in the lipid matrix.
- Phospholipids = main fabric; **proteins determine most of the membrane's functions**.
- Different cell types (and different membranes within a cell) have different protein sets.
  - e.g., >50 kinds of proteins found in the plasma membrane of red blood cells.

### Two major types of membrane proteins

| | **Integral proteins** | **Peripheral proteins** |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Penetrate the hydrophobic interior of the bilayer | **Not embedded**; loosely bound to the membrane surface |
| Details | Most are **transmembrane** (span the membrane); some extend only partway in | Often attached to exposed parts of integral proteins |
| Structure | Hydrophobic regions = nonpolar amino acids, usually coiled into **α helices**; hydrophilic parts exposed to water on both sides | n/a |

- Some integral proteins have **hydrophilic channels** for hydrophilic substances (even water).
- Example: **bacteriorhodopsin**: 7 transmembrane helices; N-terminus outside, C-terminus inside (shows directional orientation).
- Attachments give animal cells a stronger framework than the membrane alone:
  - **Cytoplasmic side**: proteins attached to the **cytoskeleton**.
  - **Extracellular side**: proteins attached to **ECM fibers**.

### Six major functions of membrane proteins (Fig. 5.7)

1. **Transport**: channel proteins (hydrophilic tunnel, selective for a solute) or carrier proteins (shuttle a substance by changing shape); some hydrolyze **ATP** to actively pump substances.
2. **Enzymatic activity**: enzyme with active site exposed to adjacent solution; may be organized in teams for sequential steps of a metabolic pathway.
3. **Signal transduction**: **receptor** with a binding site shaped for a chemical messenger (e.g., a hormone); changes shape and relays the message inside (usually by binding a cytoplasmic protein).
4. **Cell-cell recognition**: **glycoproteins** act as ID tags recognized by proteins on other cells (usually **short-lived** binding).
5. **Intercellular joining**: proteins of adjacent cells hook together (e.g., **gap junctions**, **tight junctions**); more **long-lasting** than recognition.
6. **Attachment to the cytoskeleton and ECM**: maintains cell shape, stabilizes protein location; ECM-binding proteins can coordinate extracellular and intracellular changes.

- A single cell can have proteins doing several of these; a single protein can have multiple functions → the membrane is a **functional mosaic** as well as a structural one.
- *Think:* a transmembrane protein that binds an ECM molecule can transmit a signal into the cell (combines functions (c) and (f)).

## The Role of Membrane Carbohydrates in Cell-Cell Recognition

- **Cell-cell recognition**: a cell's ability to tell one type of neighboring cell from another.
  - Important for sorting cells into **tissues and organs** in embryos.
  - Basis for **immune system rejection of foreign cells**.
- Cells recognize each other by binding **carbohydrate-containing molecules** on the extracellular surface.
- Membrane carbohydrates:
  - Short, **branched chains of <15 sugar units**.
  - Bonded to **lipids** → **glycolipids**.
  - Bonded to **proteins** → **glycoproteins** (most common).
- Vary between species, between individuals, and between cell types in one individual → act as **markers**.
- Example: **human blood types A, B, AB, O** reflect variation in carbohydrates of glycoproteins on red blood cells.

## Synthesis and Sidedness of Membranes

- Membranes have distinct **inside and outside faces**:
  - The two lipid layers can differ in composition.
  - Each protein has a directional orientation.
- Asymmetry is set up as the membrane is built by the **ER** and **Golgi**:
  1. Membrane proteins and lipids are made with the **ER**; carbohydrates are added to transmembrane proteins (→ glycoproteins) and may be modified.
  2. In the **Golgi**, glycoproteins get further carbohydrate modification; lipids acquire carbohydrates (→ glycolipids).
  3. Glycoproteins, glycolipids, and secretory proteins are transported in **vesicles** to the plasma membrane.
  4. Vesicles **fuse** with the plasma membrane (**exocytosis**): the vesicle's *inside* face becomes the *outside* (extracellular) face of the plasma membrane. Carbohydrates end up on the **extracellular** side.
- Key idea: the **extracellular face** of the plasma membrane comes from the **inside (lumen) face** of ER, Golgi, and vesicle membranes.

### Concept Check 5.1 (quick answers to think through)
1. Carbohydrates in the vesicle are on the **inside (lumen) side** of the vesicle membrane, which becomes the extracellular side.
2. Grass near **hot springs** should have **more saturated** hydrocarbon tails (prevent excess fluidity at high temp); grass in cooler soil should have **more unsaturated** tails (keep membranes fluid).

# Concept 5.2: Membrane structure results in selective permeability

- A biological membrane is a **supramolecular structure**: many molecules ordered into a higher level of organization, with **emergent properties**.
- Key emergent property: **regulating transport** across cellular boundaries.
- **Form fits function**: the fluid mosaic model explains how membranes regulate molecular traffic.

## Traffic across the plasma membrane

- A steady two-way flow of small molecules and ions. For a muscle cell:
  - **In:** sugars, amino acids, other nutrients, **O₂** (for cellular respiration).
  - **Out:** metabolic wastes, **CO₂**.
  - Also regulates inorganic ions (**Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻**) by shuttling them across.
- Despite heavy traffic, membranes are **selectively permeable**: take in some substances, exclude others.

## The Permeability of the Lipid Bilayer

| Type of molecule | Crosses the bilayer? |
|---|---|
| **Nonpolar / hydrophobic** (hydrocarbons, **CO₂**, **O₂**) | **Yes, easily**: dissolve in the lipid bilayer, no proteins needed |
| **Polar molecules** (glucose, other sugars) | **Slowly** |
| **Water** (small but polar) | Not rapidly on its own |
| **Ions** (charged, with shell of water) | Even **less** likely to penetrate |

- The hydrophobic interior **impedes** direct passage of ions and polar molecules (hydrophilic).
- The lipid bilayer is only **one part** of the gatekeeper system; **membrane proteins** also regulate transport.

## Transport Proteins

- Ions and many polar molecules can't cross on their own → they pass **through transport proteins** that span the membrane, avoiding contact with the lipid bilayer.

| | **Channel proteins** | **Carrier proteins** |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Hydrophilic **channel/tunnel** that certain molecules or ions use | **Hold onto** passengers and **change shape** to shuttle them across |
| Example | **Aquaporins** (water channels) | **Glucose transporter** in red blood cells |
| Stats | Aquaporin: 4 identical subunits; each allows **single-file** passage of up to **3 × 10⁹ water molecules/sec** | Moves glucose **50,000× faster** than unaided diffusion |

- Transport proteins are **specific** for the substance they translocate (one substance or a small group of related ones).
  - The glucose transporter even **rejects fructose** (a structural isomer of glucose).
- **Selective permeability = lipid bilayer (discriminating barrier) + specific transport proteins.**
- Next questions (set up for 5.3 and 5.4): What sets the **direction** of traffic? What **drives** molecules across? → **passive transport** vs. **active transport**.

### Concept Check 5.2 (quick answers to think through)
1. O₂ and CO₂ are **nonpolar/hydrophobic**, so they dissolve in the lipid bilayer.
2. Water is **polar**, so it crosses the hydrophobic interior slowly; **aquaporins** are needed for rapid movement of many water molecules.
3. Aquaporin selectivity is probably not about size alone: H₃O⁺ is **charged** (hydronium ion), so the channel's selectivity likely depends on **charge/chemical properties** of the pore, not just size.

## Quick Review Summary

- Membrane = **fluid mosaic** of phospholipids, proteins, and carbohydrates.
- **Fluidity** is controlled by tail saturation, cholesterol, and temperature; organisms adapt lipid composition to their environment.
- **Integral** (embedded/transmembrane) vs. **peripheral** (surface) proteins; six functions of membrane proteins.
- **Carbohydrates** (glyco-lipids/proteins) = cell-cell recognition markers, always on the **extracellular** face because of ER → Golgi → vesicle **sidedness**.
- **Selective permeability**: nonpolar molecules cross freely; polar molecules and ions need **channel** or **carrier** proteins, which are highly **specific**.
